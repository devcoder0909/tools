## What Is an EMI?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Installment. It is the scheduled amount a borrower pays to a bank or financial institution every month until the loan is repaid.

For a standard fixed-rate reducing-balance loan, the EMI generally remains unchanged throughout the agreed repayment period. However, the proportion of principal and interest within each payment changes over time.

During the initial months, a larger portion of the EMI usually goes towards interest. As the outstanding principal decreases, the interest component becomes smaller and a larger portion of each payment goes towards repaying the principal.

### What Does an EMI Include?

| EMI Component | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| Principal | The original amount borrowed from the lender |
| Interest | The borrowing cost charged by the lender |
| Monthly EMI | The scheduled payment containing principal and interest |

For example, if your monthly EMI is ₹25,000, the entire amount does not necessarily reduce your outstanding loan by ₹25,000. A portion covers the applicable interest, while the remaining amount reduces the principal.

## EMI Calculation Formula

Most standard fixed-rate reducing-balance loans use the following mathematical formula to calculate monthly Equated Monthly Installments:

$$
E = P \times r \times \frac{(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n-1}
$$

Where:

- **E — Equated Monthly Installment:** The amount payable every month.
- **P — Principal Amount:** The original loan amount borrowed.
- **r — Monthly Interest Rate:** The annual nominal interest rate divided by 12 and converted into decimal form.
- **n — Number of Monthly Installments:** The total repayment tenure expressed in months.

The monthly interest rate is calculated as:

$$
r = \frac{\text{Annual Interest Rate}}{12 \times 100}
$$

The total number of monthly installments is:

$$
n = \text{Loan Tenure in Years} \times 12
$$

### Step-by-Step EMI Calculation Example

Suppose you borrow **$100,000** for **15 years (180 months)** at an annual interest rate of **7.5%**.

The calculation works as follows:

1. Principal amount: $P = 100,000$
2. Annual interest rate: 7.5%
3. Monthly interest rate: $r = 7.5/1200 = 0.00625$
4. Loan tenure: 15 years
5. Total installments: $n = 15 \times 12 = 180$

Using the standard EMI formula:

$$
E = 100000 \times 0.00625 \times
\frac{(1.00625)^{180}}{(1.00625)^{180}-1}
$$

The estimated results are:

| Loan Detail | Amount |
| :--- | ---: |
| Loan Principal | $100,000.00 |
| Annual Interest Rate | 7.5% |
| Loan Tenure | 15 years |
| Monthly EMI | $927.01 |
| Total Repayment | $166,861.80 |
| Total Interest | $66,861.80 |

These figures are illustrative. Actual lender repayment schedules may differ slightly because of rounding, payment dates, fees, and loan-specific calculation conventions.

## Loan Tenure Impact: How Repayment Duration Changes Your EMI

Choosing the right loan tenure is an important part of financial planning. A longer repayment period generally reduces the monthly EMI but increases the total interest paid over the life of the loan.

Consider a **$100,000 loan at an annual reducing-balance interest rate of 8.5%**:

The example demonstrates an important borrowing trade-off:

- A shorter tenure means higher monthly payments but lower overall interest.
- A longer tenure means lower monthly payments but higher overall interest.
- The right tenure depends on your income, existing financial commitments, and repayment capacity.

For this example, extending the loan from 15 years to 30 years reduces the monthly EMI by approximately $215.83 but increases total interest by approximately $99,555.73.

**Important:** A lower EMI does not automatically mean a cheaper loan. Always compare both the monthly installment and total repayment amount.

## Reducing Balance vs. Flat Rate Loans

When comparing vehicle financing or consumer loans, you may encounter both reducing-balance and flat-rate interest quotations.

These methods calculate interest differently, so comparing only the advertised interest percentages can be misleading.

### 1. Reducing Balance Method

Under the reducing-balance method, interest is calculated on the outstanding principal amount.

As you make monthly payments and repay part of the principal, the remaining balance decreases. Consequently, the interest component of future installments generally becomes smaller.

This is a commonly used method for home loans, car loans, and personal loans.

### 2. Flat Rate Method

Under a flat-rate loan, interest is calculated on the original principal amount for the entire agreed tenure.

The principal may decrease as you repay the loan, but the interest calculation continues to use the original loan amount.

Therefore, a flat interest rate can represent a higher borrowing cost than its quoted percentage initially suggests.

### Head-to-Head Comparison: $20,000 Auto Loan for 5 Years

| Feature | Reducing Balance | Flat Rate |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Original Principal | $20,000 | $20,000 |
| Quoted Annual Rate | 10% reducing | 6% flat |
| Loan Tenure | 60 months | 60 months |
| Monthly Payment | $424.94 | $433.33 |
| Total Interest | $5,496.45 | $6,000.00 |
| Total Repayment | $25,496.45 | $26,000.00 |

Although the flat-rate quotation is only 6%, its equivalent annualized borrowing cost is approximately 10.85% on a nominal annual basis, or 11.40% on an effective annual basis, assuming equal monthly payments and no additional charges.

The actual APR disclosed by a lender can differ depending on fees, payment timing, and applicable disclosure rules.

**Tip:** Compare the total repayment amount and APR, not just the advertised flat interest rate.

## How to Reduce Loan Interest Through Prepayment

Prepayment means paying an additional amount towards your outstanding loan principal before the scheduled repayment date.

For eligible reducing-balance loans, an early principal prepayment can reduce the balance on which future interest is calculated.

Depending on the lender's rules, you may be able to:

- Reduce the remaining loan tenure while keeping the EMI unchanged.
- Reduce the future EMI while keeping the remaining tenure similar.
- Choose a combination of lower EMI and shorter tenure, where permitted.

### Strategy 1: Make an Additional EMI Payment

Paying an additional installment towards principal can help reduce your outstanding loan balance.

The amount of interest saved depends on when the additional payment is made, the loan's remaining tenure, and the lender's prepayment policy.

### Strategy 2: Increase Your EMI When Your Income Rises

If your income increases, you may consider increasing your monthly loan payment rather than extending your spending.

A higher payment can accelerate principal repayment and potentially reduce the overall interest cost.

### Strategy 3: Make Lump-Sum Principal Prepayments

Bonuses, incentives, savings, or other available funds may be used to make additional loan payments.

Before making a lump-sum prepayment, check whether your lender charges fees or applies minimum prepayment conditions.

### Important Prepayment Considerations

- Check whether your loan allows partial prepayment.
- Review applicable foreclosure or prepayment charges.
- Confirm whether the lender reduces the EMI or the loan tenure.
- Compare potential interest savings with other uses of your available money.
- Keep an emergency fund before using all your savings to repay debt.

Prepayment calculations are estimates. Actual savings depend on the lender's amortization method and payment adjustment rules.

## Understanding a Loan Amortization Schedule

An amortization schedule is a month-by-month breakdown of your loan repayment.

It shows how much of each installment goes towards interest, how much reduces the principal, and how much loan balance remains after each payment.

Consider a **$100,000 loan at an annual interest rate of 8% for 20 years**.

The estimated monthly EMI is $836.44.

| Month | Opening Principal | Monthly EMI | Interest | Principal Repaid | Closing Balance |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Month 1 | $100,000.00 | $836.44 | $666.67 | $169.77 | $99,830.23 |
| Month 2 | $99,830.23 | $836.44 | $665.53 | $170.91 | $99,659.32 |
| Month 3 | $99,659.32 | $836.44 | $664.40 | $172.04 | $99,487.28 |
| Month 120 | $61,542.10 | $836.44 | $410.28 | $426.16 | $61,115.94 |
| Month 240 | $830.90 | $836.44 | $5.54 | $830.90 | $0.00 |

The schedule demonstrates how the interest component gradually decreases while the principal component increases.

### Why Is More Interest Paid During the Early Months?

At the beginning of a loan, the outstanding principal is at its highest level.

Since interest is calculated on the remaining principal, the initial installments generally contain a larger interest component.

As the loan balance decreases, the monthly interest charge also decreases. More of the same scheduled EMI can then go towards repaying the principal.

The exact month when principal repayment becomes greater than interest depends on the loan amount, interest rate, EMI, and tenure.
