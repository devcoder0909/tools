## How Percentage Calculations Work: Understanding "Per Cent"

The word "percent" derives directly from the Latin phrase *per centum*, meaning "by the hundred." A percentage is simply a dimensionless ratio or fraction expressed with a denominator of 100. Whether calculating retail sales tax, analyzing stock market quarterly gains, computing tipping on a restaurant bill, or comparing academic exam scores, percentages provide a universal benchmark to evaluate proportional quantities.

## The 4 Fundamental Percentage Formulas with Mathematical Proofs

Percentage calculations fall into four mathematical categories. Understanding the specific algebraic structure behind each mode guarantees accuracy:

### Mode 1: What is X% of Y? (Percentage of a Number)
To find a given fraction of a total quantity, convert the percentage into a decimal fraction by dividing by 100, then multiply by the total base value:

$$\text{Result} = \left(\frac{X}{100}\right) \cdot Y$$

- **Real-World Example:** What is **18% sales tax** on a **$250 restaurant catering invoice**?
- **Calculation:** $\left(\frac{18}{100}\right) \times 250 = 0.18 \times 250 = \mathbf{\$45.00}$. The total bill equals $\$250 + \$45 = \$295.00$.

### Mode 2: X is What Percentage of Y? (Ratio to Percentage)
To determine what proportion one number represents relative to another, divide the numerator part by the denominator whole, then multiply by 100:

$$\text{Percentage} = \left(\frac{X}{Y}\right) \cdot 100$$

- **Real-World Example:** If a student answers **46 questions correctly out of 50** on an exam:
- **Calculation:** $\left(\frac{46}{50}\right) \times 100 = 0.92 \times 100 = \mathbf{92.0\%}$ (Grade A).

### Mode 3: Percentage Increase or Decrease (Percentage Change)
To measure how much a metric has grown or declined relative to its original starting baseline:

$$\text{Percentage Change} = \left(\frac{V_{\text{final}} - V_{\text{initial}}}{|V_{\text{initial}}|}\right) \cdot 100$$

- **Positive Result:** Indicates a **percentage increase** (growth, profit, inflation).
- **Negative Result:** Indicates a **percentage decrease** (markdown, loss, weight reduction).
- **Real-World Example:** A company's monthly recurring revenue (MRR) rises from **$12,500** in March to **$15,000** in April:
- **Calculation:** $\left(\frac{15,000 - 12,500}{12,500}\right) \times 100 = \left(\frac{2,500}{12,500}\right) \times 100 = 0.20 \times 100 = \mathbf{+20.0\% \text{ Increase}}$.

### Mode 4: Percentage Difference (Comparing Two Values Without Direction)
When comparing two distinct measurements where neither value serves as an "initial" baseline (for example, comparing the fuel efficiency of two car models), calculate the difference relative to the average:

$$\text{Percentage Difference} = \left(\frac{|A - B|}{\frac{A + B}{2}}\right) \cdot 100$$

- **Real-World Example:** Company A produces **120 units** and Company B produces **150 units**:
- **Calculation:** $\frac{|120 - 150|}{(120 + 150) / 2} \times 100 = \frac{30}{135} \times 100 = \mathbf{22.22\% \text{ Difference}}$.

## Retail Discounts, Markdowns & Sales Tax Calculations

Shoppers and retail managers frequently navigate percentage markdowns and price reductions:

### 1. Calculating Final Sale Price After Discount
To calculate final price after an advertised discount percentage:

$$\text{Final Price} = \text{Original Price} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{\text{Discount \%}}{100}\right)$$

$$\text{Amount Saved} = \text{Original Price} \cdot \left(\frac{\text{Discount \%}}{100}\right)$$

- **Example:** A winter jacket priced at **$180** is on sale for **35% off**:
- **Final Price:** $\$180 \times (1 - 0.35) = \$180 \times 0.65 = \mathbf{\$117.00}$.
- **Money Saved:** $\$180 \times 0.35 = \mathbf{\$63.00}$.

### 2. Warning: Consecutive (Stacking) Discounts Do Not Add Up!
A common retail misconception is that taking an "extra 20% off already 30% discounted merchandise" equals 50% off. In reality, discounts apply sequentially:
- Starting price: **$100.00**
- First 30% discount: $\$100 \times 0.70 = \mathbf{\$70.00}$
- Second 20% discount on the remaining $70: $\$70 \times 0.80 = \mathbf{\$56.00}$
- **True Effective Discount:** A final price of $56.00 represents a **44% total discount**, not 50%!

## Markup vs. Margin: The Classic Business Trap

In commerce, confusing **markup** and **gross profit margin** is one of the most frequent causes of pricing failure in retail businesses:

| Feature / Metric | Markup Percentage | Gross Profit Margin |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mathematical Basis** | Computed relative to **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** | Computed relative to **Final Selling Price (Revenue)** |
| **Formula** | $\text{Markup} = \frac{\text{Price} - \text{Cost}}{\text{Cost}} \times 100$ | $\text{Margin} = \frac{\text{Price} - \text{Cost}}{\text{Price}} \times 100$ |
| **Example Cost** | $100.00 | $100.00 |
| **Example Price** | $133.33 | $133.33 |
| **Resulting Metric** | **33.33% Markup** | **25.00% Margin** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> If your product costs $100 and you need a 25% profit margin, adding a 25% markup produces a selling price of $125. However, your actual profit margin is only $\frac{25}{125} = 20.0\%$, leaving your business 5% short of its margin target! To achieve a true 25% margin, the required price is $\frac{\$100}{1 - 0.25} = \mathbf{\$133.33}$.

## Reverse Percentage Calculations: Finding the Original Value

Reverse percentages are essential when calculating original pre-tax costs or baseline prices from receipts:

### Case A: Finding Original Price Before a Discount
If you purchased a discounted item for **$68.00** after a **15% discount**:

$$\text{Original Price} = \frac{\text{Final Discounted Price}}{1 - \frac{\text{Discount}}{100}} = \frac{68}{1 - 0.15} = \frac{68}{0.85} = \mathbf{\$80.00}$$

### Case B: Finding Pre-Tax Price from a Total Receipt
If your final receipt total is **$108.00** including **8.0% sales tax**:

$$\text{Original Pre-Tax Price} = \frac{\text{Total With Tax}}{1 + \frac{\text{Tax Rate}}{100}} = \frac{108}{1 + 0.08} = \frac{108}{1.08} = \mathbf{\$100.00}$$

Notice that you *cannot* simply subtract 8% from $108 ($108 - $8.64 = $99.36), which is mathematically incorrect because the tax was levied on the original base, not the final total.

## Mental Math Shortcuts: The Commutative Property of Percentages

Did you know that percentages are completely reversible? In mathematics, multiplication is commutative:

$$x\% \text{ of } y = y\% \text{ of } x$$

$$\frac{x}{100} \cdot y = \frac{x \cdot y}{100} = \frac{y}{100} \cdot x$$

### Rapid Mental Trick Examples
- **What is 16% of 50?**
  Calculating 16% in your head is tricky, but reversing the problem makes it effortless: **50% of 16** is simply half of 16 = **8**!
- **What is 72% of 25?**
  Flip it: **25% of 72** is one quarter of 72 = **18**!
- **What is 18% of 200?**
  Flip it: **200% of 18** is double 18 = **36**!

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I calculate the percentage of a number?
Multiply the base number by the percentage rate divided by 100. For example, to find 20% of 150, compute $(20 \div 100) \times 150 = 0.20 \times 150 = 30$.

### What is the percentage change formula?
Subtract the initial value from the final value, divide by the absolute initial value, and multiply by 100: $\left(\frac{\text{New} - \text{Old}}{|\text{Old}|}\right) \times 100$. A positive answer indicates growth, while a negative answer indicates a decline.

### How do I calculate a retail discount or sales markdown?
Multiply the original price by $(1 - \frac{\text{Discount}}{100})$. For instance, an item originally costing $120 with a 15% discount has a final price of $\$120 \times (1 - 0.15) = \$120 \times 0.85 = \$102.00$, saving you $18.00.

### How do I find what percentage one number is of another?
Divide the part by the total whole and multiply by 100: $\left(\frac{\text{Part}}{\text{Whole}}\right) \times 100$. For example, scoring 45 points out of 180 total points equals $(45 \div 180) \times 100 = 25.0\%$.

### Can percentage change be negative?
Yes. A negative percentage change indicates a decrease, such as a drop in product prices, reduced expenditure, or population decline.
